## Observed problems

- Homepage: search is prominent, but the remaining first screen is dominated by a wide promotional carousel and a dense grid of category tiles. Similar categories—PCs, laptops, hard drives, beauty—repeat without a clear progression toward a decision.
- Homepage: location and sign-in prompts obscure content during the initial experience.
- Results: the sidebar offers substantial filtering power, but it is long, visually dense, and mixes high-value filters with niche options.
- Results: product titles are verbose and inconsistent. Comparable attributes—battery life, form factor, noise cancellation, microphone quality—must be extracted manually from titles and subtitles.

- Product detail: the delivery-location failure appeared both in the central content and purchase panel.
- Product detail: “About this item” contained several long marketing bullets, making key trade-offs difficult to scan.

### Hypotheses requiring validation

- Shoppers in specification-heavy categories use multiple tabs, notes or external reviews because Amazon does not keep shortlisted products in one decision surface.
- Normalized attributes would reduce back-and-forth between search results and product pages.
- A goal-led homepage could accelerate discovery, but only if its recommendations are sufficiently relevant.
- Review summaries could increase confidence, but untraceable AI-generated claims would create a trust problem.

## Three product directions

### 1. Product comparison tray — recommended

**User problem:** Shoppers cannot preserve context while comparing similar products. Titles and seller-authored bullets use inconsistent terminology.

**Interaction:** Add a “Compare” checkbox to result cards and the product page. Selecting 2–4 products opens a persistent bottom tray. “Compare now” expands into a responsive matrix with normalized rows such as price, rating, battery, noise control, form factor, delivery and return status. Users can highlight differences, remove products, and open a product without losing the comparison.

**Visual opportunity:** A restrained tray with thumbnails, price and selection count; a clean table with a sticky attribute column, clear difference highlighting, and one primary action per product.

**Customer benefit:** Faster, more confident decisions with less tab switching and memory work.

**Implementation effort:** Medium—approximately 10–14 hours for a polished frontend prototype using seeded product data and local state.

**Trade-offs:** Attribute normalization is category-specific. A real product would require a dependable catalog schema, and the UI must remain usable on mobile.

### 2. Decision-ready search results

**User problem:** Results provide abundant information but poor scanability. Product differences are buried inside titles and changing card formats.

**Interaction:** Give every result a consistent card structure: concise name, price, delivery, rating and three query-relevant attributes. Replace the longest sidebar sections with a sticky row of active filter chips while retaining a collapsible advanced-filter drawer. Keep sponsored and deal labels explicit.

**Visual opportunity:** Stronger typography, aligned key-value rows, consistent image ratios and clearer separation of product facts from promotions.

**Customer benefit:** Faster elimination of unsuitable products and easier visual comparison before opening details.

**Implementation effort:** Low–medium—approximately 6–10 hours.

**Trade-offs:** Less distinctive than comparison. Choosing the wrong “key” attributes could hide information important to specialist shoppers.

### 3. Intent-led homepage discovery

**User problem:** The signed-out homepage prioritizes merchandising categories rather than the shopper’s current goal.

**Interaction:** Preserve search, then add a compact “Help me choose” entry point with missions such as “Headphones for travel,” “Upgrade my workspace,” or “Gifts under $50.” Each mission asks one or two constraints and produces a focused result set. Promotions remain below this decision layer.

**Visual opportunity:** A calmer first screen with fewer, larger choices, purposeful imagery and progressive disclosure instead of repeated four-tile category grids.

**Customer benefit:** Provides a useful starting point for shoppers who know their need but not the correct query or product category.

**Implementation effort:** Medium—approximately 8–12 hours for one complete guided path.

**Trade-offs:** Highest merchandising and recommendation risk. It demonstrates attractive UI, but less of the core evaluation-and-purchase workflow.
